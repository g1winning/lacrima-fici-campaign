Research: pricing, GST and margins

Prepared 24 October 2026 for the Romeo family (Shepparton, Victoria). Proposed trade price: 200 mL bottle, $7.50 wholesale, case of 12 at $90, recommended retail $12.00 (one earlier email said $12.50). Current direct-to-consumer (DTC) site: 250 mL at $14.95, case of 12 at $149.95.

Every web finding below carries a URL. Unless stated otherwise, "retrieved" means retrieved on 24 October 2026. Anything I could not confirm from a primary page is marked UNVERIFIED. Where I have made an assumption or a calculation of my own, I say so.

1. GST on fig syrup

Finding

A fig syrup sold as a table syrup, topping or flavouring for food is very likely GST-free. The ATO detailed food list treats the closest comparators as GST-free condiments, and treats syrups for flavouring drinks as taxable. The deciding test is the "essential character" of the product, which turns on how it is marketed, labelled and packaged. So the label and the website copy decide the GST status.

What the ATO says

Supporting ATO pages (both "last updated 26 February 2026", retrieved 24 October 2026):

  • GST-free food: lists "spices, seasonings and condiments" and "spreads for bread (such as jam and peanut butter)" as GST-free, and states "the way food items are marketed affects their GST classification" (promotion, labelling including the name, instructions, packaging, display). https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/gst-excise-and-indirect-taxes/gst/in-detail/your-industry/gst-and-food/gst-free-food
  • Taxable food: lists confectionery, flavoured water, non-carbonated drinks under 90% juice, and so on. Syrups are not listed as taxable except via the beverage rule. https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/gst-excise-and-indirect-taxes/gst/in-detail/your-industry/gst-and-food/taxable-food

What this means in practice

  1. There is no entry for "fig syrup" or "fruit syrup" as such. I am reasoning by analogy to maple syrup, golden syrup and "syrups for flavouring food". That is a reasonable reading, but it is an inference, not an ATO ruling on this product. Mark as: likely GST-free, not formally confirmed.
  2. The risk is positioning. If the label or site says "cocktail syrup", "mixer", "for spritz", "for soda" or "for coffee", the ATO's entry 778 can pull the product into taxable. Keep the label and the website on food uses: pancakes, yoghurt, cheese boards, roast meats, dressings, desserts. A single secondary line such as "stir into a drink" is a risk to avoid, since 778 applies even where food use is subsidiary.
  3. Cordial is not the right comparator. A reduced fig syrup is not a cordial, and if it were treated as one, the 90% juice test would apply.
  4. For certainty, the Romeo family could ask the ATO for a private ruling or confirm with their accountant, quoting entries 779, 799 and 778. UNVERIFIED: I have not confirmed the current cost or turnaround of an ATO private ruling.
  5. Being GST-free does not stop a business claiming GST credits on its packaging and kitchen hire if it is registered for GST (threshold $75,000 turnover, UNVERIFIED here, check with ATO or accountant).

Decision for the margin maths

Base case: GST-free. The $12.00 RRP is the shelf price with no GST inside it, and the $7.50 wholesale has no GST on it. The GST-inclusive case is run below as the downside scenario.

2. Margin maths

Definitions. Retailer gross margin % = (ex-GST selling price minus wholesale cost) divided by ex-GST selling price. Wholesale is $7.50 ex-GST per 200 mL bottle ($90 per case of 12). In the GST-inclusive case the shelf price includes 10% GST, so the retailer's ex-GST revenue is the RRP divided by 1.1.

2a. Retailer margin at $7.50 wholesale

Scenario Shelf price Retailer ex-GST revenue Retailer gross margin $ Retailer gross margin %
GST-free $12.00 $12.00 $4.50 37.5%
GST-free $12.50 $12.50 $5.00 40.0%

Read-across: if GST-free, $12.00 gives independents 37.5%, and $12.50 gives exactly 40%. If the product turned out taxable, $12.00 falls to 31.3%, below the 35% to 45% most independents look for (see the benchmarks note below).

4d. Yield: fresh figs per litre of reduced syrup

I found no published yield for reduced fig syrup or fig petimezi. What I can say:

  • Raw figs are mostly water. Norwegian food composition data gives 86 g water and 9.5 g sugars per 100 g (https://www.matvaretabellen.no/en/figs-raw/, retrieved 24 Oct 2026). USDA data, from memory and UNVERIFIED here, gives about 79 g water and about 16 g sugars per 100 g, so sugar content varies by variety and ripeness.
  • Calculation (mine): reduce fig juice to a syrup of around 70% solids, assuming 65% of the sugar is recovered after pressing and straining. Using 9.5 to 16 g of sugar per 100 g gives roughly 9 to 15 g of syrup per 100 g of fruit, which is 0.07 to 0.11 litre per kg at a syrup density of about 1.35. That is roughly 9 to 15 kg of figs per litre. Using whole puree with no loss, 16 g sugar gives about 6 kg per litre. Working range: 6 to 15 kg per litre, central 8 to 10 kg per litre. UNVERIFIED, treat as an order-of-magnitude estimate to be replaced by a test batch.
  • Commercial evidence of dilution with another sugar source: Maggie Beer's Fig Vino Cotto is reported to contain concentrated grape juice (54%), figs (31%) and a red wine vinegar preservative (reported in a search result for https://shop.maggiebeer.com.au/?p=1326 and not re-opened, UNVERIFIED). Most commercial "fig syrups" are extended with sugar or must.

At 8 kg per litre, a 200 mL bottle uses about 1.6 kg of figs.

The recipe only works at $7.50 wholesale with the family's own surplus or second-grade fruit at a low internal cost, or with an extended recipe.

Food safety certification

  • Victorian base layer. Council registration, and for Class 2 a Food Safety Program audited and approved (see section 4c). This is the HACCP-style foundation.

Country of Origin and the "Australian Made" kangaroo

  • Australian food sold in Australia must carry the mandatory Country of Origin Food Labelling (the government kangaroo and bar chart label, or text such as "Made in Australia from at least X% Australian ingredients"). There is no licence fee and no registration to use these. Source: Australian Government, Country of Origin Food Labelling style guide, https://business.gov.au/-/media/grants-and-programs/cool/label-artwork/country-of-origin-labelling-style-guide-pdf.pdf (search result, retrieved 24 Oct 2026); and Food and Agribusiness network note, https://foodagribusiness.org.au/opportunities/free-use-of-the-made-in-australia-food-labelling-mark/ (retrieved 24 Oct 2026).
  • The Australian Made Campaign says food and drink sold within Australia do not need to be licensed with it and that it is not involved in the food label. It no longer issues new licences for the Australian Made logo on food and beverage products sold in Australia, and licenses only products for export. https://australianmade.com.au/for-business/food-labelling/ and the Food and Agribusiness note above (both retrieved 24 Oct 2026).
  • If the licence were available: the annual fee is based on sales of the licensed products, minimum $300 plus GST ($330 inc GST) for sales up to $300,000, then $400 up to $500,000, $600 up to $750,000, $800 up to $1 million. https://australianmade.com.au/for-business/how-much-does-it-cost/ and https://australianmade.com.au/why-buy-australian-made/faqs (retrieved 24 Oct 2026).
  • So for a domestic fig syrup the free government label is the route. The claim wording depends on ingredients: "Product of Australia" needs essentially all ingredients and processing to be Australian. If the recipe uses imported sugar or lemon juice, the label is likely "Made in Australia from at least X% Australian ingredients". Confirm with the ACCC guidance. https://www.accc.gov.au/business/advertising-and-promotions/country-of-origin-claims

For a new supplier to Coles or Woolworths, one advisory source says trading terms can strip 15% to 30% off the headline wholesale price once rebates, promotions, and similar are included (https://www.mvretailadvisory.com.au/blog/supermarket-trade-terms-explained, undated, UNVERIFIED). What this means for the pitch

Recommendation: $7.50 wholesale and $12.00 retail works for independents, but not for the majors or a distributor.

Fig syrup sold as a table syrup is very likely GST-free (ATO entries 779 and 799), so independents earn 37.5% at $12.00 and a clean 40% at $12.50. Use $12.50 as the pitch RRP. Keep cocktail and drink claims off the label, because ATO entry 778 makes drink syrups taxable.

Coles, Woolworths terms or a 20% to 25% distributor cut the farm's net to roughly $5.25 to $6.38, which is at or below mid-case costs. Retailer margin on the distributor path falls to 17% to 25%.

The real cost risk is the fruit. Bought at market prices, figs cost more than the shelf price. Use own or second-grade fruit, test the yield, and quote glass in volume. Freight is about $2.06 to $2.45 a bottle, so set minimum orders. Do not let the DTC case price undercut shops.
