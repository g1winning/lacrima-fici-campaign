Four creative platforms

Four routes are set out below. Each is a different answer to the one question a buyer or a shopper asks in the two seconds they give a new bottle: why this, instead of the maple I already buy? The recommendation sits at the end. The label is already designed around the first route, which weighs in its favour, but the other three carry arguments the first cannot, so the recommendation borrows from them rather than discarding them.

Route A. Tear of the Fig

The name on the label is Latin for the tear of the fig. A ripe Black fig, left on the tree a day past the point a supermarket would accept it, splits at the eye and weeps a bead of syrup. The product is that moment, pressed. Everything in this route is quiet, premium, Italian in its manners. The photography is a single fig, a single drop, cream linen, deep aubergine shadow. The typography is the label's Cormorant Garamond.

Headlines. "Picked at the point of weeping." "A fig, the moment it gives in." "Shepparton, pressed." "Nothing added. Nothing could be." "The tear of the fig, on your pancakes."

Body copy, consumer. "Every February the Romeo family's Black figs ripen in the Goulburn Valley heat until they split and weep. That bead of syrup is where this bottle starts. The figs are crushed, warmed gently until they thicken, bottled. That is the whole method. Pour it anywhere you would pour maple."

Where it works. Fine-food grocers, David Jones Food, Harris Farm, delis, gift, the food press. It gives journalists an image and a sentence, which is most of what a product story needs.

Where it strains. A Coles or Woolworths category manager is reading a margin line, not a poem. On its own this route does not say "table syrup" fast enough for a mass shelf.

Route B. Anywhere you'd pour maple

The practical route. Australia imported 3,731 tonnes of maple syrup in 2025, a fifth more than in 2024, and all of it is imported, so the pure-syrup shelf in every supermarket is a shelf of Canadian product sold near cost. Fig syrup from Shepparton is the Australian answer: the same uses, a cleaner and less dominating sweetness, a single ingredient, grown 180 kilometres from Melbourne, at a price that earns the shelf its margin.

Headlines. "Anywhere you'd pour maple." "The pure syrup grown in Victoria." "Pancakes have a local option now." "Less sweet than it looks." "Made from one thing. Figs."

Body copy, trade. "Maple imports rose a fifth last year and every bottle is sold near cost. Lacrima Fici gives the shelf the Australian pure syrup beside them, at a margin: 200 mL, $12.00 recommended retail, $7.50 wholesale in cases of 12, $4.50 a bottle to the retailer, delivered from mid February 2027. One ingredient, no preservatives, 12 months ambient, refrigerate after opening."

Where it works. Buyers. Shelf talkers. Catalogue. Anything where the shopper is already standing in front of maple.

Where it strains. Pure maple sells at $2.20 to $3.80 per 100 mL in Coles and Woolworths; Lacrima Fici is $6.00. The route must never imply price parity. The Figgery on Kangaroo Island already pitches "fig instead of maple", so the idea is not unclaimed, though its reach is South Australian. Comparative claims need care: "less dominating" is a taste description and is fine, "better for you" is not without testing. The route also borrows its meaning from maple, which keeps the fig in second place.

Route C. The whole crop

The origin story on the current website: for years the Romeos watched perfect figs with a sun spot or a split skin go to waste because the fresh-fruit spec rejected them. The syrup was invented to use the whole crop. Food writers reach for this story; the "ugly fruit" idea has carried brands in juice, cider and chutney.

Headlines. "The figs the shelf refused." "Too ripe to travel. Just right to press." "Every fig we grow, sold." "Sun spots welcome."

Where it works. PR, podcasts, the farm lunch, Instagram. The story has a villain (the spec), a hero (the family) and a resolution (the bottle). Dirty Linen and Deep in the Weeds are built on exactly this kind of producer story.

Where it strains. Telling a supermarket buyer their fresh-fruit spec is the villain is a poor opening line. For trade this route is reworded as "whole-crop" and "nothing wasted", which buyers now have sustainability targets against.

Route D. Eighty summers

Provenance. The Romeo family have grown figs on the same ground at Central Avenue, Shepparton, since 1944. Three generations, one orchard, one ingredient. The photography is the orchard, the hands, the copper pot, the long table under the trees.

Headlines. "Figs on the same ground since 1944." "Three generations. One ingredient." "A Shepparton family's table syrup."

Where it works. Local and regional press, Ritchies and IGA shoppers in Victoria, the Melbourne Food and Wine Festival audience, gift season. It is also the quiet proof behind every other route.

Where it strains. Every second artisan brand in the country claims heritage. On its own it does not tell anyone what to do with the bottle.

Recommendation

Run one platform, not four. Tear of the Fig is the brand, because it is on the label, it is distinctive, and it gives the press an image. Under it, each audience gets the line it needs. The buyer gets Route B's proposition, "the pure syrup grown in Victoria", with the margin and the delivery date in the same breath. The shopper standing at the maple shelf gets "Anywhere you'd pour maple" on the shelf talker. The food writer gets Route C's whole-crop story with Route D's orchard as the setting. Nothing in that arrangement fights the label.

The executive team's decision is narrower than choosing between four routes. It is whether to accept this layered arrangement, or to pick a single line and run it everywhere. The single-line version is cheaper to produce and easier to brief; the layered version sells harder in each channel. The package is built on the layered version so that either choice can be made from it.
