The strategy in full

Prepared for Santo Romeo and the Romeo family, Shepparton, 24 October 2026. A working draft for the executive marketing team to argue with. Every figure below traces to one of the four research documents in this package, each with its source. Anything those documents could not confirm is marked unverified there, so it is kept out of the copy here.

The short version

Lacrima Fici is pure Black fig syrup from an orchard the Romeo family has farmed at Shepparton since 1944. It goes to retail in a 200 mL flask bottle at $7.50 wholesale in cases of 12, with a recommended shelf price of $12.00, and first deliveries in the week of 15 February 2027, which is the first week of the Victorian fig harvest. The campaign has two halves. From 16 November 2026 a bottle lands on the desk of forty named buyers, with a founder's voice on LinkedIn and a story in the trade press behind it. From 15 February 2027 shoppers meet the syrup on a spoon in the stores which said yes, in the food pages during fig season, then at a lunch under the trees at Shepparton in the middle of the Melbourne Food and Wine Festival. The whole plan costs about $19,000 in hard costs, with a $12,500 version and a $35,000 version beside it.

Three facts from the research shape everything else. Australia imported 3,731 tonnes of maple syrup in 2025, a fifth more than in 2024, almost a third more than in 2021. The pure-syrup shelf is growing with no Australian line on it. Pure maple sells at $2.20 to $3.80 per 100 mL in Coles or Woolworths, which puts a $6.00 per 100 mL fig syrup at two and a half times the shelf. The pitch cannot be price. It has to be margin, provenance, use. The realistic February listings are independents: Ritchies, IGA stores buying direct, gourmet grocers. Coles with Woolworths range by calendar once or twice a year, with terms stripping 15 to 30 per cent from a wholesale price. The majors get the box in November so the relationship starts, but the plan does not depend on them for February.

What is being sold

One ingredient. Ripe Black figs, crushed, warmed until they thicken, bottled. Twelve months ambient, refrigerate after opening. The label is the Lacrima Fici design already approved: cream ground, the botanical fig plate, the patterned border, Cormorant Garamond, "Tear of the Fig" beneath the name. The bottle is the 200 mL flask.

The name on the current website is Fyrup, with the line "God's Fig Syrup". The label carries Lacrima Fici. Retail cannot launch with two names. A buyer who opens the box, then finds a different brand on the website, stops there. The website needs the Lacrima Fici name, the trade page and the corrected shelf price before 16 November. The religious line can stay in the family story on the website. It does not go on trade material.

The current website also sells a 250 mL bottle at $14.95 and a case of twelve for $149.95. The single bottle is priced at 5.98 cents a millilitre, which matches the proposed shelf price almost exactly and will not upset a retailer. The case price works out at $10.00 for a 200 mL equivalent, 17 per cent under the shelf, and a shop owner who finds it will complain. The case either rises to shelf parity or is renamed a gift case and priced accordingly, before any buyer sees the site.

The price, and what it returns to a retailer

The pricing in the brief moved between $12.50 in the first email and $12.00 in the second. Both are costed here.

Fig syrup sold as a table syrup is very likely GST-free. The Australian Taxation Office's detailed food list treats syrups for flavouring food, maple syrup among them, as GST-free condiments, with syrups for flavouring beverages taxable, judged on how the product is marketed. There is no entry for fig syrup, so this is reasoning by analogy for the family's accountant to confirm, but the consequence is clear enough to act on: the label stays on food uses, the website too, while the cocktail idea is a conversation with Dan Murphy's buyers, never a line on the pack.

Independent grocers and delis commonly look for 35 to 45 per cent; the Australian primary evidence for that is thin, but the published category margin expectations at Coles in one Senate submission ran from 39.5 to 45 per cent. At $12.00 the independent earns 37.5 per cent, which is acceptable. At $12.50 the number is a round 40 per cent, which is the figure a buyer wants to see on a sell sheet. The recommendation is $12.50, and it is the first decision for the executive team.

The margin argument against maple is in dollars, not percentages. A supermarket earning its published 28 per cent group gross margin on a $6.00 own-brand pure maple makes about $1.68 a bottle. The same facing holding Lacrima Fici at $12.00 returns $4.50. That is the line for the sell sheet and the LinkedIn posts.

A distributor does not fit at this price. A 20 per cent distributor margin on top of $7.50 leaves the retailer 22 per cent at a $12.00 shelf, or takes the farm to $6.00 a bottle if the retailer's price is held. The plan therefore sells direct to independents, with a fortnightly Melbourne delivery run through the season, and sets freight at cost on orders under four cases. Australia Post charges $24.70 to $29.45 to send a 12-bottle case interstate, which is $2.06 to $2.45 a bottle, so a single case shipped free costs the farm a third of the case.

The cost of goods is the family's business, but one point from the research belongs here. Bought at market prices of $10 to $15 a kilogram, the figs alone would cost more than the shelf price, because a pure reduction needs something like six to fifteen kilograms of fruit for a litre of syrup. The recipe works at $7.50 only because the fruit is the family's own second-grade crop, with no market value as fresh fruit. That is the economic truth under the "whole crop" story, and it is worth saying plainly to Santo.

The market a buyer is looking at

The buyer for table syrups has one growing line and it is imported. Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada's trade data shows Australian maple imports at 2,840 tonnes in 2021, 3,112 tonnes in 2024 and 3,731 tonnes in 2025, worth C$40.9 million. Australia is the fourth largest maple importer in the world by volume. In October 2026 Canadian press reported Quebec's strategic maple reserve nearly empty, with the last stock expected to sell out by the end of February 2027; if maple prices rise in Australia, the gap to fig narrows. That is an inference, not a forecast, and it should be used as colour, not a claim.

There is no fig syrup on a national supermarket shelf that the research could find. The Australian fig syrups are small regional makers: The Figgery on Kangaroo Island, whose 250 mL Sticky Fig Syrup sells at $14.90 and is stocked in South Australian independents and in Maggie Beer's hampers; Hillside Family Grove at Gulgong, 250 mL at $16.00; Rosnay Organic at Canowindra, a spiced 375 mL at $15.00. Per 100 mL, Lacrima Fici at $6.00 sits level with The Figgery at $5.96 and just under Hillside at $6.40. Maggie Beer's own fig vino cotto, a grape-must product with figs, is $20.50 for 250 mL. Nobody is making a Victorian fig syrup, and nobody is on a Coles or Woolworths shelf. "Black figs from Shepparton" is an open claim.

Two cautions from the competitor work. The Figgery already pitches itself as a fig alternative to maple, so "fig instead of maple" is not an unclaimed idea, though its reach is South Australian. And a retailer page describes The Figgery as "no added sugar" while the brand's own Instagram says it is made with Australian sugar. Lacrima Fici's single-ingredient list is a sharper claim than any competitor can make, provided it stays true in the recipe.

There are no sales statistics for fig syrup as a category in Australia. The brief asked for them and the answer is that none exist in any scan data the research could reach; the category is too small to be measured. Tell the buyer so, in those words, then show the maple import line with the price ladder.

Who the retail buyers really are

The brief names Coles Group, Woolworths, Ritchies, IGA, Dan Murphy's and Thirsty Camel. The research tested each.

Coles prefers that new suppliers hold a RangeMe profile, which its category buyers search ahead of range reviews; a direct approach is allowed and should be paired with the profile. Coles Local has twelve Victorian stores that range premium local lines, but there is no separate ranging program; it is the standard Coles route with a local story. Woolworths takes submissions through its Partner Hub against a Range Review Calendar that sits behind a login, with reviews once or twice a year by sub-category and off-cycle listings only in exceptional cases. Both need a GS1 barcode, council registration, country of origin labelling, signed trading terms before a first delivery. Both publish group gross margins of about 28 per cent, with higher expectations on specialty lines. The majors are a 2027 range review, not a February delivery. The box still goes to them in November because the relationship takes a year to build and because a buyer who has tasted the product reads the RangeMe profile differently.

Metcash supplies about three quarters of what IGA stores sell, but its own submission to the ACCC says IGA retailers are free to buy from local suppliers direct, with a strong preference for local producers in fresh and specialty lines. Ritchies trades under the IGA banner, is Victorian, and has no public supplier page, so it is a direct approach to the group's buying office. Direct-to-store IGA and Ritchies are the most realistic Victorian listings for February. The Metcash expression of interest is lodged as well, after its portal reopens on 26 October.

Harris Farm Markets has a gourmet grocery buyer inbox and a stated preference for local product, but every store is in New South Wales, Queensland or Canberra. It is a good New South Wales target and should get a box, with no expectation of Victorian shelf.

Dan Murphy's does range a cordials-and-syrups category, Monin among them, with mixer editorial beside it, so a fig syrup for an old fashioned has a plausible home there through the Endeavour Partner Portal. The GST point above means this is a separate conversation about a separate use, not the lead. Thirsty Camel sits in Metcash's liquor banners, reaching mainly South Australia with the Northern Territory; there is no evidence any of those banners range food syrups. One box, low expectations.

One name is missing from the brief. The Shepparton News ran a story on 23 April 2024, "Giving a fig for heritage", about Figtasia Farms at Shepparton East, run by a Santo Romeo and his son Joel, with about 5,400 trees and nearly thirty years of supplying fresh figs to Aldi in Sydney and Melbourne. If that is this family, Aldi already knows them, and Aldi's Special Buys program is a listing route the brief leaves out. Confirm with Santo before anything is built on it.

The trade campaign, 16 November 2026 to 12 February 2027

The proposition to a buyer is one sentence: the Australian pure syrup for a shelf that is entirely imported, returning $4.50 a bottle against $1.68 on own-brand maple, delivered from the first week of the harvest.

The centrepiece is the buyer's box. One 200 mL bottle, a small brass spoon, a card in Santo's hand that says pour this over whatever is in the fridge tonight, and the sell sheet with the three numbers. Forty boxes, posted on 16 and 17 November so they land before the buyers' Christmas lockdown in the third week of December. Australia Post charges $16.00 for a 500 gram to one kilogram parcel, so forty boxes cost about $640 in postage, roughly $1,600 all up with box, spoon, card. Twenty go to the named retailers above; twenty go to Victorian delis, gourmet grocers and the specialty distributors who supply them. Every box is followed by a call from Santo ten days later. The box is a sample; the call is the range review date.

Behind the box runs Santo's own voice on LinkedIn. Six posts, written for a category manager scrolling in work hours, weekly from 16 November then fortnightly through January, with the first, third, fifth promoted to buying titles at the named retailers. The audience is a few hundred people, so a sponsored budget of $1,500 at the global planning rate of around $10 a click buys the right eyes rather than many eyes. One Sales Navigator seat at A$154.99 a month for three months finds the names.

The trade press gets a release on 16 November with a bottle behind every pitch: Inside FMCG, Retail World, Australian Food News, Food and Drink Business, where Kim Berry's team takes story ideas by email, Hospitality Magazine for the cheese angle. Trade and founder podcasts, Eat Drink Innovate among them, are offered a conversation with Santo in November or January; thirty minutes of a grower talking is reused in social for a year.

Everything points at a trade page on the website carrying the sell sheet, the specification, the barcodes and the shelf artwork, because the buyer's assistant will look for case dimensions at four on a Friday afternoon. From 16 November the brand's Instagram posts three times a week, Facebook with it, with $300 a month behind the best posts. Every buyer looks at the Instagram account before replying. Forty posts with real engagement is proof of demand; an empty account is a reason to say no.

The second round of boxes, to anyone who did not answer and to any new names, goes out the week of 18 January, with follow-up calls through to 12 February.

The consumer campaign, 15 February to 28 March 2027

The consumer campaign is six weeks long because fig season is. Shrove Tuesday falls on 9 February, a week before launch, and is used as a teaser; Easter Sunday is 28 March and closes the window. Both dates correct the brief.

In the stores which ranged the syrup, a demonstrator stands at the shelf on Saturdays from 20 February with thick yoghurt under a thread of syrup on a spoon, then a cube of blue cheese for the second taste. Twelve tasting days across Ritchies and IGA stores in Melbourne and the Goulburn Valley, costed at $350 a day with product; a family member behind the table halves that. A shelf talker under every facing reads "Anywhere you'd pour maple". A recipe card goes home with every taster, with every online order too. Tasting is the one medium that converts on the spot, and fig syrup on blue cheese is the pairing that converts.

The press work rides fig season, which is in every food section in February. Twenty-five kits (a bottle, a kilogram of the harvest, a story card) go on 8 February to the writers the research found reachable: Dan Cunningham at Broadsheet, who says in print he takes food products, with Laura Box, Broadsheet's Melbourne food editor; Kara Monssen at the Herald Sun; Kylie Imeson at Country Style, whose readers prefer Australian-made by a wide margin; Good Food, Gourmet Traveller, delicious.; Rushani Epa at Time Out Melbourne; then the independent writers Richard Cornish, Dani Valent, Jill Dupleix, Lee Tran Lam, approached as journalists with a short email first, never an unannounced parcel.

The podcasts built on producer stories are the best fit the research found. The Producers and Dirty Linen are both reached through one Deep in the Weeds contact form; Every Bite on ABC Radio National with Matt Preston runs culture-through-food episodes where a fig history fits; Triple R's Eat It takes producer interviews on Sunday lunchtimes for the cost of an email.

The single biggest consumer idea is a lunch at the orchard. Saturday 13 March, inside the Melbourne Food and Wine Festival's 5 to 21 March window though not an official event: twenty seats for writers, podcasters, the buyers who said yes; a coach from Melbourne; figs picked from the tree; the copper pot on the stove; a long table under the trees with the syrup on the cheese, in the glaze, in the glass. It costs about $3,500 with the coach. The orchard in harvest is the one asset no competitor can copy, and one day there produces more coverage than a month of emails.

Twenty-five Victorian recipe creators with 10,000 to 80,000 followers are seeded from 1 February, with four paid recipe reels at around $400 each; the Australian micro tier publishes at $100 to $500 a post. Two thousand dollars of Instagram and Facebook advertising runs from 15 February, geo-targeted to Victoria at a planning rate of A$12 to A$18 per thousand impressions, with one job: tell people within driving distance of a ranging store that the bottle exists, in the six weeks it is in season. The local press costs nothing; it reaches the Ritchies or IGA shopper first: Shepparton News, which has already written about the farm, the Shepparton Adviser, ABC Goulburn Murray, farm-gate weekends through March, a stall at TatFest in Tatura on Sunday 7 March.

SBS is held as an option rather than bought. SBS Media publishes no minimum spend or rate card; a quote is requested for a small SBS On Demand test in Victoria and judged in the extended budget. The SBS guidelines treat paid product integration as third-party funding needing formal approval, and bar any "as seen on SBS" claim, so the plan cannot be built on a pantry appearance. Product is offered to The Cook Up's producers as a fig-season ingredient, which costs a bottle. Adam Liaw, at 320,000 followers, takes brand work through a formal route with a fee attached. Justine Schofield, who the brief most likely means by Jackie Schofield, has 203,000 followers with SBS On Demand series behind her; she is approached as a gifted send. Neither is in a low budget.

Creative

Four routes are set out in the creative platforms document: Tear of the Fig, which is the label; Anywhere you'd pour maple, the practical substitution line; The whole crop, the story of the figs the fresh-fruit specification rejected; and Eighty summers, the 1944 provenance. The recommendation is to run Tear of the Fig as the brand, give the buyer the maple-shelf proposition with the margin in the same breath, give the shopper "Anywhere you'd pour maple" on the shelf talker, and give the press the whole-crop story set in the orchard. The executive team's decision is whether to accept that layered arrangement or pick one line for everything.

The imagery is built in one world: deep aubergine, cream linen, antique gold, sage green, soft window light, the fig weeping its single drop. Twenty-eight frames were rendered for this package. They appear throughout the presentation, listed in the image plan with a note on which a photographer would re-shoot if the budget allows.

The media plan and the budget

The matrix in the presentation lays out every tactic with its dates, its cost, whether it is paid, earned or owned, then the reason Santo would do it. Hard costs come to about $19,000: around $5,000 for the trade campaign, around $14,000 for shoppers. The minimal version at $12,500 drops the paid creator posts, halves the tasting days by putting family behind the table, and sends writers to the harvest lunch under their own steam. The extended version at $35,000 adds a quoted SBS On Demand test, twelve more tasting days, a paid Inside FMCG feature, a stand at C&I Expo on 16 and 17 March, an Australian Food Awards entry in the syrup class in mid-2027, and a Fine Food Australia stand in Sydney in September 2027.

Agency time, the family's time and the cost of about ninety sample bottles are not in those figures.

Decisions for the executive team

The shelf price: $12.00 as in the second email, or $12.50 as in the first and as the margin maths recommends. Everything in the package is written at $12.00; it changes to $12.50 with one find-and-replace.

The brand name across every surface: Lacrima Fici on the label, therefore on the website, the trade page, the social accounts, before 16 November.

The direct-to-consumer case price, which undercuts the proposed shelf by 17 per cent.

The creative arrangement: one platform with audience-specific lines, or one line everywhere.

Channel priority: independents first with the majors as a 2027 range review, as recommended, or a harder push at Coles and Woolworths for February against the evidence of their calendars and terms.

The cocktail angle: kept as a Dan Murphy's conversation off the label, as recommended, or dropped entirely to protect the GST-free position.

The harvest lunch: on 13 March as proposed, with the coach, or a smaller version without.

Risks and what is not yet verified

The GST position is an inference from the ATO food list, not a ruling on this product. The fruit cost only works with the family's own second-grade crop; a bad season or a decision to buy fruit breaks the price. The majors' calendars may not open until late 2027. Freight on single cases eats a third of the case. The Figgery occupies the maple-alternative idea in South Australia. No glycaemic or health claim can be made without testing the actual syrup, and the website's current line that diabetics can enjoy it has to come down before any buyer reads it. The link between this family and the Figtasia Farms story is unconfirmed. Editor names in the research were current in October 2026 and move.
